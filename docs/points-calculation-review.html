Scoring & Combo Review

Where the points actually come from in Dubai Missile Command, why skill barely moves the number, and what 16 candidate combo rules do about it.

Branch claude/points-calculation-review-yej8oy · 240 instrumented headless runs + 200 re-scored runs · generated from src/headless/score-attrib.ts and src/headless/combo-eval.ts

01The headline

The score is a wave counter wearing a different label.

Four bot skill presets, upgrade build held constant so the comparison is aim and reaction only. Median score rises cleanly from novice to perfect — but almost all of that rise is survival, not skill expression. Fit score against final wave and the quadratic explains — of the variance. Two runs that die on the same wave land within a few percent of each other regardless of how they were played.

The core problem. Skill is priced almost entirely through longevity. A player who shoots twice as accurately earns 7% more per kill; they score higher only because accuracy buys them more waves, and the waves pay a flat, escalating stipend. The score therefore duplicates a number already on screen (wave reached) instead of measuring how well the city was defended.

02Where the points come from

Between 44% and 70% of every run is income no amount of skill changes.

Two awards make up the flat block, and both are indexed on the wave number rather than on anything the player did:

  • Buildings survived — 100 × buildingsAlive × wave, evaluated at each wave-end bonus screen. With 12 scenic buildings that is up to 1,200 × wave per wave.
  • Wave clear — 250 × wave, in game-sim.ts at the wave-complete branch.

Because each is linear in the wave index, the cumulative total is quadratic in waves survived. Clearing wave 11 with the city intact pays 14,450 points for the act of clearing it. That is the "each next wave just gives more points" effect, and it is the single largest term in the game.

The confound cuts toward overstating separation, so the near-zero gap observed is if anything a conservative reading — but it means the right conclusion is drawn from the R² and points-per-kill figures, not from this chart alone.

03Three leaks in the current rules

1. The combo multiplier pays out on kills the player did not make

Combo is built purely by the player: processRootExplosionCombo increments only on a playerCaused root explosion that killed something, and resets to 1 on a whiff. But the award itself, g.score += getKillReward(t) * g.combo, runs at every kill site — Patriot, Phalanx, Hornets, Roadrunner, Iron Beam, EMP and flares included.

Combo amplification — points that exist only because the multiplier was above x1

Measured across 100 runs: 29–40% of all combo-generated points land on automated kills. For a perfect run that is roughly 11,900 points per game of the player's multiplier being spent on their own turrets.

2. Multi-kill bonuses are not gated on the player at all

In game-sim.ts the DOUBLE / TRIPLE / MEGA KILL awards (150 / 350 / 700) fire on any explosion reaching 2+ kills. Only the multiShots statistic checks playerCaused; the points do not. A Patriot's blast radius harvests MEGA KILLs on its own, and multikill:hornet plus multikill:patriot come to 8.9% of a perfect run's score.

This one reads as a straight bug rather than a balance choice, and is worth fixing regardless of what happens to the rest of the scoring.

3. Automation is paid at the player's rate

The player personally kills 63–73% of threats; automated systems take the rest and are paid identically per kill — on top of already paying the player by keeping them alive into higher-value waves. That is the same contribution counted twice.

Bonus finding: build choice outweighs aim

With the shipped presets rather than a fixed build, average outscores good — 69,960 against 54,296. The average bot buys EMP and Patriot early; the good bot buys Hornets first and Patriot last. Every tier comparison in this report holds the build constant for exactly this reason, but in real play the effect is live: it is a plausible explanation for "I played better and the number went down".

04Does the rhythm premise hold up?

The proposal was that landing three shots in quick succession is harder than three spread out. It is — and the data also says something awkward about rewarding it.

Hit rate by the gap since the previous shot

A "hit" is a player blast that killed at least one threat. Gaps measured within a wave, within a run. All player blasts across 50 runs per tier.

Hover for hit rate and sample size per bucket. One threshold applied to every series: buckets holding fewer than 250 shots are omitted. For the three weaker tiers that removes the 60–120 and 120+ buckets, which carry only 4–168 shots each and are dominated by end-of-wave cleanup rather than by rhythm; plotting them would read as a steep trend where there is only noise.

Within a player, the premise is confirmed and the effect is large. A perfect-tier run hits 47% of the time when firing inside 15 ticks of the previous shot, 66% at 15–30 ticks, 85% at 30–60, and 99.7% when more than a second has passed (n = 9,750 / 8,821 / 2,407 / 598). The same gradient shows up in the average and good tiers across the well-sampled 0–60 tick range: 35% → 45% → 67% and 41% → 49% → 61%. Firing fast genuinely costs accuracy, and the effect is worth roughly 20–40 percentage points of hit rate.

But rapid fire is the regime where skill separates least. In the 0–15 tick bucket the four tiers land at 50.3%, 34.8%, 41.2% and 47.2% — not merely close but out of order, with novice nominally top. Separation only appears further right, and even there only perfect stands clear (84.7% at 30–60 ticks); average and good are interleaved at 67.4% and 60.8%. A bonus that paid purely for firing fast would reward the one regime that does not discriminate at all.

Read the cross-tier comparison with care, because it is confounded by construction: the presets differ in minClickInterval and maxBurstShots, so the novice bot almost never fires inside 15 ticks (n = 582) while the perfect bot does it constantly (n = 9,750) and under harder pressure. The tiers are not sampling the same rhythm regime. The within-tier gradient is the solid finding here; the cross-tier ordering is suggestive at best, and the safe conclusion is the weak one — fast shooting is harder, but this data does not show it to be a skill discriminator.

The design reading: frame a rhythm bonus as sustained pressure — hits that keep landing while the tempo is high — rather than as fast fingers. And note that the game already has the mechanical substrate for this. player-fire-limiter.ts runs a burst-charge magazine (2 per live launcher, 4 with Double Magazine) with a catch-up recharge curve of 30 → 10 → 5 ticks, where firing resets the streak. Holding fire and then spending a full magazine is already a distinct, skilled act. It is simply not scored.

05Cash out at 3, or at 5?

The threshold question has a clean empirical answer, and it is not the one that maximises skill separation.

How far consecutive-hit streaks actually get

Share of streaks reaching at least N consecutive hitting shots. A streak ends on any blast that kills nothing.

Dashed guides mark the two proposed thresholds.

Player blasts whiff often — even at perfect play the blast hit rate is only about 55%, so streaks break constantly. That sets hard limits on any consecutive-hit mechanic:

  • Five is effectively expert-only. It fires 0.0 times per wave for a novice and 0.1 for an average player — those players would finish a whole run without the mechanic ever triggering, and would never learn it exists. Only 1–3% of a non-expert's streaks reach five at all.
  • Three is reachable but still not frequent at the low end: 0.7 cash-outs per wave for a novice, 1.3 for good, 3.9 for perfect. That is enough to be discovered and enough to feel like a reward, which five is not.
  • Five separates skill better precisely because it is rare — 13.5x novice→perfect spread against 10.4x for three, on the reformed base. Rarity and legibility are in direct tension, and the threshold choice is exactly that trade.

The resolution is not to choose. Pay at 3 so the mechanic is legible, and let the payout escalate on each consecutive cash-out so that chaining to 6, 9 and beyond carries the separation that a bare 5-threshold would. That is the cashout-3-escalating and rhythm-3-chain rules below.

06Sixteen combo rules, measured

Every rule re-scored against the same 200 recorded runs, on an equal points budget.

Why this is exact rather than a guess. g.combo is read only at the five score-award sites, the HUD, and the maxCombo stat — never in spawning, targeting, damage or the fire economy. A different combo rule could not have changed how any recorded run played out, so re-scoring an event log offline gives the same answer as re-simulating. Every rule is also calibrated to spend the same total combo budget as the current one across the dataset, so the comparison isolates the shape of the mechanic rather than how generous its constant happens to be.

Score base:

What each column means
  • nov / avg / good / perf — median total score for that tier under this rule.
  • spread — perfect ÷ novice. How much the score separates the best player from the worst.
  • same-wave — within runs that ended on the same wave, best tier ÷ worst tier. This is the number that says whether the score measures skill rather than longevity. 1.00x means it does not.
  • combo % — how much of a perfect run's score the combo term accounts for.
  • acc corr — correlation between the combo term and the player's accuracy within a single tier. Pooling across tiers just re-measures the tier gap, so this is computed inside each tier and averaged. It answers: among players of similar ability, does the mechanic reward the more accurate one?
  • fires/wave — how often the player is shown a payoff. Legibility.

07What the numbers say

Combo redesign alone cannot fix this. On the base as it ships, every one of the sixteen rules lands between 8.4x and 10.3x skill spread, and same-wave separation between 1.03x and 1.12x. The combo term is only ~17% of a perfect run's score; the flat wave income drowns it. Change the combo rule on its own and the scoreboard will look essentially the same.

On the reformed base — flat income cut, automated kills at 0.45x, multi-kill bonuses player-gated — the combo term becomes ~30% of the score and the choice of rule starts to matter: spread ranges 8.5x to 13.5x, same-wave 1.06x to 1.23x. Fix the base first; then the combo rule is worth arguing about.

Recommended order of work

  1. bug Gate multi-kill bonuses on ex.playerCaused. Smallest change here, and defensible on its own terms.
  2. step 1 Stop the combo leak — apply g.combo only to kills from player-caused explosions. This is the specific thing that felt wrong, and it is a contained change at five award sites.
  3. step 2 De-index the flat bonuses from the wave counter. Tie the wave bonus to the threat value actually faced × the fraction of the city still standing, so it grows with difficulty but collapses when the player leaks. This is the change that does the heavy lifting.
  4. step 3 Pay automated kills at ~0.45x. Also turns "beat your own turrets to the target" into a real skill expression, since it is already a race.
  5. step 4 Then pick the combo rule, from the shortlist below.

The combo shortlist

Three defensible answers depending on what the mechanic should feel like:

  • rhythm-3-chain — cash out every 3 hits, value scaled by how tightly the three landed and by how many cash-outs have been chained without a miss. Best same-wave separation among the frequent-feedback rules (1.172x), fires 3.9x/wave. This is the closest to the original proposal and it survives measurement.
  • escalating-mult — keep today's per-shot multiplier feel (fires 22x/wave, constant feedback) but make the ladder superlinear so the top rungs are worth disproportionately more. 12.07x spread, and it preserves the fine-grained accuracy sensitivity that discrete cash-outs lose.
  • cashout-5 / rhythm-5 — the highest skill separation (13.5x, same-wave up to 1.23x), but fires about once per wave. Choose this only if the score is meant to be a leaderboard instrument rather than moment-to-moment feedback.

The rules to avoid are the forgiving rolling windows (window-3, window-4), where misses do not reset. They fire most often but discriminate worst on every measure — 8.5x spread, 0.26–0.33 accuracy correlation. Forgiveness and skill expression are directly opposed here.

08Limits of this analysis

  • No cloud replays. The capture worker gates /api/sessions behind CAPTURE_BEARER_TOKEN, which was not available. Everything here is measured against the headless bot presets. The harnesses accept real recorded runs unchanged, so pointing them at the archive is a credentials problem, not a tooling one.
  • Bots are not people. The four presets model skill through aim jitter, reaction delay, click interval and peripheral attention. They are a reasonable ladder but they do not reproduce human pacing, tilt, or the way a person reads a wave. The rapid-fire confound in section 04 is a direct consequence.
  • Same-wave separation is selection-confounded in both directions, as noted in section 02. It is reported because it is the right question, not because the estimate is clean.
  • The --reform base is a sketch, not a proposal. Its constants (flat income ÷ 3, automation at 0.45x) were chosen to be roughly plausible, so it shows the shape of what happens when the flat term stops dominating, not a tuned design.
  • Not modelled: the incentive to skip upgrades. Paying automated kills less creates a reason not to buy turrets. Upgrades would need to stay clearly net-positive for survival; upgrade-sweep.ts is the tool to check that before shipping any of this.
